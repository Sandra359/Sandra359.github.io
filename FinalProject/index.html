00 · The Dataset

About the Data

1,028,034 individual residential sales, where every transaction contributes to the story.

This analysis draws on three data sources. The foundation is a dataset of 1,028,034 residential housing transactions recorded across all 98 Danish municipalities between 2008 and 2024, sourced from Kaggle. From this, we derive variables such as mean square metre price, transaction volume, and average property size per municipality per year.

Municipal income data comes from Statistics Denmark's income registers, providing annual average disposable household income at the municipality level. Population figures come from the same source, tracking demographic shifts across every municipality.

Together, these three sources allow us to trace not just what happened to prices, but what it meant for people's lives — whether they could afford to buy, where they chose to live, and how the gap between Denmark's most and least accessible places has evolved over sixteen years.

01 · The National Picture

Can Danes Still Afford to Buy a Home?

The national affordability index peaked in 2014 and has been declining ever since.

Housing is one of the most fundamental parts of life. It shapes where you live, how much you spend, and what your future looks like. But over the past two decades, the answer to whether a typical Dane can afford to buy a home has become increasingly complicated.

Figure 1 shows the national average affordability index across all 98 Danish municipalities from 2008 to 2024. In 2008, Denmark was in the midst of the global financial crisis. House prices had run far ahead of incomes in the years prior, and the affordability index sat at its lowest point in our dataset, around 19.5 [1]

As the crisis unwound, house prices fell and incomes slowly recovered. By 2014, affordability had climbed to its peak at around 24.9, a roughly 25% improvement in just six years. For a brief window, Denmark was accessible again.

From 2015 onward, affordability began a slow decline as prices kept rising faster than incomes. Then in 2021 and 2022, interest rates rose sharply after years near zero. Rather than pushing affordability further down, the rate rise dampened buyer demand so much that transaction volumes collapsed and nominal house prices began to adjust downward. This paradoxically allowed the affordability index to recover modestly, reaching around 23.7 by 2024 [2].

The brief window of accessibility that opened after the financial crisis has been slowly closing ever since.

Fig. 1 · National average affordability index (income / sqm price), 2008–2024

02 · The Mechanism

The Interest Rate Story Nobody Talks About

Much of the 2010s affordability improvement was built on borrowed time. When rates rose sharply from 2022 onward, the market froze — but with a counterintuitive result.

Figure 2a shows the relationship between the affordability index and nominal interest rates. The green line tracks affordability and the red dashed line tracks interest rates. From 2013 to 2021, nominal interest rates were at zero, and affordability plateaued at a relatively high level. Danes had become accustomed to extraordinarily cheap borrowing.

The affordability improvement of the 2010s was not earned — it was lent.

The underlying tension remains. Even as affordability has partially recovered, house prices have grown faster than incomes every year since 2014. The zero-rate era masked this gap — cheap borrowing made expensive homes manageable. Now that borrowing costs have returned to more normal levels, the gap between what people earn and what housing costs is once again visible.

Fig. 2a · Affordability index vs. nominal interest rate %, 2008–2024

Figure 2b shows annual transaction volume alongside the affordability index. Transactions climbed steadily from around 35,000 in 2008 to nearly 120,000 by 2021. Then from 2022, as borrowing costs rose sharply, transactions fell by roughly 40% in a single year. The drop in demand put downward pressure on nominal house prices, which paradoxically allowed the affordability index to recover modestly from 2023 onward. By 2024, transaction volumes remain well below their 2021 peak.

Fig. 2b · Annual transaction volume vs. affordability index, 2008–2024. The 2022 rate rise caused transaction volumes to collapse, which dampened prices and allowed affordability to partially recover.

03 · Regional Divergence

Five Denmarks, Not One

The Capital Region's affordability line sits so far below the others it almost looks like a different country.

~14.7 Capital Region · 2024 Lowest affordability index in Denmark
~28 North Jutland · 2024 Consistently among the most affordable
2× Urban–Rural gap Your money goes nearly twice as far outside Copenhagen

A national average is useful, but it can also be misleading. Figure 3 shows the affordability index for each of Denmark's five regions from 2008 to 2024. What appears as a single national trend fractures into something far more complicated and far more unequal.

North Jutland started as the most affordable region in 2008, and has consistently ranked among the highest, reaching almost 29 in 2014 and around 28 by 2024. Likewise, Zealand Region also ranks among the highest, and reaches the highest overall peak, (above 29), in 2013. Central Jutland and Southern Denmark sit in the middle, generally between 23 and 27 throughout the period, despite in 2023 where Sothern Denmark reaches the highest score.

And then there is the Capital Region. Its line sits so far below the others that it almost looks like it belongs to a different country. While other regions generally stayed between 23 and 29, the Capital Region started at around 15 in 2008, briefly recovered to around 19 by 2012, and has since declined steadily to around 14.7 by 2024.

In practice this means that a household in North Jutland with the same income as one near Copenhagen can afford nearly twice as much housing. Two Danes, similar jobs, similar salaries, but one can afford a family home and the other is priced out. The data shows this gap has only widened over the past sixteen years.

Fig. 3 · Population-weighted affordability index by region, 2008–2024

04 · The Great Divide

Two Housing Markets in One Country

The gap between urban and rural affordability has persisted throughout the entire period — and both markets have gotten harder since 2014.

Figure 4 strips away regional complexity and reveals a single clean pattern: Denmark has two housing markets. Rural municipalities have consistently been more affordable than urban ones throughout the entire period from 2008 to 2024.

Rural affordability peaked around 2013 at 29, before declining gradually to 25 by 2022, and slightly recover to nearly 26.5 by 2024. Urban affordability peaked earlier, around 2012 at around 23, and has declined to around 20 by 2021 and increased to 21.5 by 2024. Housing in rural areas remains genuinely more accessible.

The gap narrowed not because urban affordability improved, but because rural affordability also fell.

The bars show the rural-urban gap each year. The gap was already present in 2008 at around 3.7 index points, and expanded to its widest between 2013 and 2017, reaching 6 to 7 index points. Since then it has narrowed somewhat, but not because urban affordability improved. It narrowed because rural affordability also fell. Both markets are getting harder, and the gap between what people earn and what housing costs continues to widen in both.

Fig. 4 · Population-Weighted Affordability: Urban vs Rural Gap, 2008–2024

05 · The Real Cost

The Price of Living in a City

Measured from the 2014 affordability peak, the gap shows how far incomes have fallen behind housing costs in the decade since.

Figure 5 makes the affordability gap concrete. Rather than showing the index itself, it answers a specific question: compared to 2014, the year affordability peaked, how much additional annual income would a household need today to have the same housing affordability as at that baseline?

In 2008, both urban and rural households needed significantly more income than in 2014 to match that baseline, reflecting how unaffordable housing was during the financial crisis. By 2014, the gap closes to zero for both groups, as this is the reference point.

From 2015 onward, the gap reopens and grows steadily. By 2022, rural households would have needed around 93,000 DKK more per year to match 2014 affordability levels, while urban households faced a gap of around 70,000 DKK. After 2022, both gaps narrow somewhat as nominal house prices adjusted downward in response to falling demand.

Based on our data, the average Danish income in 2024 is around 463,000 DKK. Closing the rural gap would require earning roughly 15% more, and the urban gap roughly 8% more, just to match what was normal in 2014.

This is the number behind the headlines about young people struggling to buy homes. It is not about lifestyle choices. It is a measurable gap between incomes and housing costs that has persisted and grown for a decade.

Fig. 5 · Additional annual income (DKK/year) required to maintain 2014 affordability levels, urban vs. rural, 2008–2024

06 · The Migration Paradox

People Moved Toward Expensive Cities Anyway

Measured from the 2014 affordability peak, the pattern is unambiguous: the places people fled to were exactly the places that had become least affordable.

If housing is so much cheaper in rural Denmark, why are not more people moving there? Figure 6a plots each municipality's affordability score in 2014 against its population growth over the following decade. Each bubble represents one municipality, coloured by region and sized by 2024 population.

If housing costs drove migration decisions, we would expect a positive relationship: the more affordable a place, the more people would move there. The trend line tells the opposite story. The most affordable municipalities in 2014, mostly small rural communities in Jutland, were precisely the ones that lost population over the next ten years. The least affordable, concentrated in and around Copenhagen and Aarhus, kept growing. Double-clicking a region in the legend isolates it, and single-clicking toggles individual regions on and off.

Fig. 6a · Affordability in 2014 (baseline) vs. population growth 2014–2024, by municipality. Bubble size = population.

Figure 6b groups municipalities by how much their affordability changed between 2014 and 2024. The pattern is striking: municipalities where affordability improved the most, the "Large gain" group, have a median population growth below zero, meaning more than half of these municipalities actually lost residents. In contrast, municipalities where affordability fell the most sharply, the "Large decline" group, show a median population growth of around 6%.

People do not move to where housing is cheap. They move to where opportunities are.

The pattern is counterintuitive but consistent: people absorb high housing costs as the price of access to jobs, education, and social networks. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle. Demand in cities keeps prices high, affordability keeps falling, but people keep moving there anyway. Cheap housing, it turns out, is not enough of a draw on its own.

07 · The Affordability Gap Up Close

Where You Can Still Afford to Live

Frederiksberg scores 9. Vejen scores 56. That is a gap of more than six to one.

The municipal rankings, illustrated in Figure 7, make the inequality concrete and personal. In 2024, the most affordable municipality in Denmark is Vejen, with an affordability index of 56.4. The least affordable? Frederiksberg, at just 9. That is a gap of more than six to one.

The top ten most affordable places share a profile: they are rural, relatively small, located in Jutland or on the smaller islands. These are also mostly communities that have struggled with population decline and economic stagnation, [3] but if you want to own a home without devoting your entire income to it, they offer something Copenhagen simply cannot.

The ten least affordable municipalities tell the opposite story: Frederiksberg, Copenhagen, Rødovre, Hvidovre, Gladsaxe, Tårnby are all municipalities tightly clustered in and around the capital. Aarhus, Denmark's second city, also makes the least affordable list.

The places with the best jobs and culture are exactly where ordinary people are being squeezed out of homeownership.

Use the animation to scroll back through time. In 2008, the picture was more compressed. Watch how the gap more or less widens year by year. The divergence is not an accident, but it is the cumulative result of two decades of uneven growth.

08 · Prices, Incomes & the Geography of Inequality

From the Peak: Ten Years of Divergence Across Denmark

Measured from 2014, when affordability was at its best, the map shows who gained and who fell behind. Green in rural Jutland. Red around Copenhagen. The income story is almost flat, with one outlier. The price story is anything but.

Figure 8 shows four layers of change across all 98 municipalities, all measured from the 2014 affordability peak. Toggle between the layers using the buttons above the map.

The population growth layer shows a mean growth of 3.9% across all municipalities. The strongest growth is concentrated around Copenhagen, with Høje-Taastrup (19.0%), Vallensbæk (18.7%), and Rødovre (18.5%) at the top. At the other end, Lolland (-8.9%), Lemvig (-8.7%), and Ærø (-6.4%) lost the most residents, a pattern consistent with the migration paradox explored in section 06.

The income growth layer shows that every municipality saw incomes rise between 2014 and 2024, with a median increase of around 30%. Vejen stands out as a clear outlier at 81%, far above the next highest, Gentofte at 53% and Copenhagen at 47%. However, rising average income does not necessarily mean that ordinary households have become richer. If income growth is concentrated among the highest earners within a municipality, the average rises while typical residents see little change. Internal inequality within municipalities is not captured by this map [4].

The sqm price growth layer shows a median increase of around 50% across all municipalities, but with extreme variation. Samsø recorded the highest price growth at 168%, followed by Ringsted at 126%. Only four municipalities saw sqm prices fall slightly between 2014 and 2024: Kolding (-3.2%), Morsø (-2.4%), Lolland (-2.4%), and Ærø (-1.3%). Every other municipality recorded price increases. The highest price growth is not exclusively concentrated around Copenhagen, since several smaller municipalities across Jutland and the islands also recorded very sharp increases, suggesting that the post-2014 price surge affected the entire country unevenly.

Incomes grew broadly. Prices grew unevenly. And the gap between the two widened most sharply exactly where people most want to live.

The affordability change layer brings the two together. The mean affordability change across all municipalities was -2.05 index points, meaning most places became slightly less affordable since 2014. Ringsted (-24.3) and Samsø (-22.8) recorded the sharpest declines, driven by their extreme price surges. Vejen (22.7) and Morsø (11.5) improved the most, but Vejen's improvement is likely driven by its unusual income growth rather than falling prices. Most of rural Jutland shows yellow tones on the map, meaning affordability held roughly flat. The area around Copenhagen turns orange and red, where price growth overwhelmed income gains. The places gaining the most people are largely the same places where affordability deteriorated most.

Fig. 8 · Municipal-level growth map (2014–2024). Toggle between Population Growth, Affordability Change, Income Growth, and sqm Price Growth using the buttons above the map.

09 · Conclusion

A Country Quietly Divided

The distribution of affordability has flattened, the tails have grown longer, and the gap between the most and least affordable municipalities has never been wider.

Figure 9 shows the distribution of affordability across all 98 municipalities in four selected years. In 2008, municipalities clustered tightly around a low affordability value of around 20, with a sharp and narrow peak reflecting how compressed the market was during the financial crisis.

From 2014 onward, the distribution shifted rightward and flattened considerably. The 2014, 2019, and 2024 curves look broadly similar, all peaking around 25 to 28, but with notably longer tails in both directions. This means that while the typical municipality has maintained a similar affordability level since 2014, the spread between the most and least affordable places has grown. Some municipalities have pulled far ahead, while others have fallen far behind. It is a divergence that is not visible in the national average alone.

Fig. 9 · Distribution of affordability across municipalities, selected years.

This analysis has traced Denmark's housing affordability story across seventeen years, 98 municipalities, and three connected datasets: house prices, incomes, and population. The picture that emerges is consistent across every lens we applied.

Affordability peaked nationally in 2014 and has declined since. The zero-rate era of 2013 to 2021 masked a structural gap between income growth and house price growth. When rates rose from 2022, transaction volumes collapsed, but the resulting price adjustment allowed affordability to partially recover, revealing just how dependent the market had become on cheap borrowing.

The divide between urban and rural municipalities is real and persistent. The Capital Region sits in a category of its own, with affordability roughly half that of the most affordable regions. And while rural municipalities remain more accessible in absolute terms, their affordability has actually declined more sharply since the 2014 peak than urban municipalities. It was a counterintuitive finding that reflects how rapidly house prices have risen even in areas far from the major cities.

Denmark is not unique in this. Cities across the wealthy world are grappling with the same divergence between urban and rural housing markets [5]. But the Danish case is striking precisely because Denmark prides itself on equality and a social contract that offers everyone a fair share of prosperity [6]. The data suggests that contract is under pressure. Not broken, but bent in ways that will shape the next generation's geography, opportunity, and quality of life. Whether this divergence is an acceptable consequence of economic geography, or a policy failure that demands a response, is a question the data alone cannot answer.
