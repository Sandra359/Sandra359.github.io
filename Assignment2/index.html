About the Data

A Decade of Incident Reports

This analysis is based on the San Francisco Police Department's (SFPD) publicly available incident report database, focusing on recorded crime activity from 2015 to 2025. The dataset covers all officially reported incidents across the city's ten police districts, capturing everything from larceny theft to violent offenses.

Each record includes the type of incident, when it occurred, and where it happened. These details make it possible to analyze not just how much crime occurs, but where it clusters and when it tends to happen — both of which are important for understanding behavioral patterns.

Crime data reflects what police record — not what actually happens. As Richardson et al. (2019) document, enforcement patterns shape the data itself: over-policed neighborhoods generate more incidents, not necessarily more crime. Keep this in mind when interpreting the patterns below.

When COVID-19 hit in 2020, many types of crime declined sharply across San Francisco as daily life came to a halt. But not all crime followed this pattern. Some categories stood out — most notably burglary, which increased instead of falling. Closely tied to routine activity and environmental conditions, burglary offers a unique lens for understanding how the disruption of everyday life reshaped crime in the city.

Summary

Three Things the Data Reveals

Before diving into the visualizations, here is a high-level synthesis of the patterns that emerge most clearly from the data.

01
Overall crime dropped during COVID-19

Total incident counts dropped approximately 20% from 2019 to 2020 — the largest single-year decline in the entire period. That suggests the pandemic had an enormous impact on crime tendencies across San Francisco.

02
Burglary moved in the opposite direction

Burglary incidents moved counter to the trend, rising 52.7% citywide from 2019 to 2020. The Northern district almost doubled its burglary count in a single year.

03
The spike was temporary

After peaking during the pandemic, burglary declined rapidly and reached its lowest recorded level by 2025 — suggesting a return toward pre-pandemic patterns as normal routines resumed.

Not just San Francisco

"Several studies have found declines in residential burglary and increases in commercial burglary following COVID-19 lockdown orders across several cities in different states."

- Yim & Riddell (2022), citing Abrams (2021); Ashby (2020); Carter & Turner (2021)

Visualizations

Exploring the Patterns

The three figures below zoom in on the story from different angles. First by comparing how crime types diverged during COVID, then by mapping where burglary concentrated across the city, and finally by breaking down when it tended to happen.

Figure 1 — Trend Overview
Crime Trends Reveal a Divergence

This figure shows how different crime types changed relative to their pre-COVID levels. Most crimes declined significantly in 2020, while burglary and motor vehicle theft spiked. This highlights that burglary behaved differently from overall crime during the pandemic.

How to read this chart: Each line represents one crime type, indexed so that its average before COVID equals 100 (dashed line). A value of 150 means 50% above the pre-COVID average; a value of 75 means 25% below. The grey band marks the COVID period (≈2020-2021). Lines above 100 during this period rose relative to their historical norm, and lines below fell.

The chart reveals a clear split. When COVID-19 hit in 2020, most crime types fell drastically below their pre-COVID baseline: assault, robbery, and larceny theft all dropped well below 100. But burglary and motor vehicle theft moved in the opposite direction, spiking far above their historical averages.

Burglary peaked at roughly double its pre-COVID average in mid-2020 and again in late 2021, before declining consistently. By the end of 2025, it had dropped to an index of approximately 60, which is the lowest level in the entire period. Motor vehicle theft showed a more modest spike during COVID, but remained elevated well after restrictions lifted, peaking again around 2024 before converging toward the same index as burglary. The remaining crime types stayed persistently below their pre-COVID averages throughout, suggesting that the pandemic accelerated a longer-term structural shift in SF crime patterns.

Figure 2 — Geographic Distribution
Burglary Hotspots Across Districts

This heatmap shows the concentration of burglary across San Francisco’s police districts before, during, and after COVID. The Northern district experienced the largest increase during the pandemic, while Tenderloin remained relatively low. It illustrates how commercial areas and local context influenced burglary patterns.

How to interact: Use the buttons to switch between Pre-COVID, COVID, and Post-COVID periods. Darker regions indicate more burglaries per day. The colour scale is normalized across all three periods. This indicates that the same colour value represents the same intensity regardless of which period you are viewing, so comparisons between periods are consistent.

Burglary in San Francisco has never been evenly spread across the city. Instead, it clusters in a handful of districts, painting a vivid map of opportunity and vulnerability. The Northern district consistently stands out as the city’s burglary hotspot, while the Tenderloin, despite its reputation for street-level crime, quietly maintains the lowest rates (Wikipedia, Tenderloin, San Francisco).

Before the pandemic, the Southern and Northern districts saw the most burglaries, averaging 2.28 and 2.57 incidents per day. These areas cover much of the city’s commercial heart. Northern Station stretches over vibrant mixed-use neighborhoods like Hayes Valley, Polk Gulch, and the Marina, while Southern’s SoMa district houses tech companies, museums, and the Moscone Convention Center (Northern Station, Southern's SoMa). Here, Routine Activity Theory (Cohen & Felson, 1979) comes alive: bustling offices, shops, and parked cars create predictable targets, while busy streets let burglars move unseen. Central and Mission districts followed closely, while Bayview and Taraval were more moderate. The rest of the city remained relatively quiet in comparison.

Then COVID-19 hit, streets emptied and offices closed, contributing to a shift in burglary patterns. The Northern district more than doubled its daily incidents to 5.29, while Central and Mission surged by 66% and 85%, reaching 3.51 and 3.19 incidents per day. Southern also saw a substantial rise to 3.32. Even districts that had been quieter, like Park, experienced spikes, though more modestly. The only exception was Bayview, largely industrial and residential, which saw a decline, likely because commercial targets were scarce (SF Examiner, 2023).

By 2021, as restrictions eased, burglary began to retreat. Northern fell back to 2.79 daily incidents, almost its pre-COVID level. Central and Richmond dropped to 2.12 and 1.33, while Southern stabilized just below its previous rate at 2.20. Yet not all districts returned fully to baseline—Tenderloin lingered slightly higher at 0.80, and Mission at 2.42—indicating that the pandemic had a lasting effect on crime in San Francisco.

Tenderloin stands out as a story of resilience. Despite social vulnerabilities, it consistently records the lowest burglary rates (0.65 pre-COVID, 1.06 during, 0.80 post-COVID). Its high density of social services and long-term residents keeps streets active and potential targets scarce (Wikipedia, Tenderloin, San Francisco). Even as burglary surged in other neighborhoods during the pandemic, Tenderloin remained remarkably resistant.

Figure 3 — Temporal Pattern
When Burglary Happens: Hour by Hour

This chart shows burglary incidents by hour of the day across pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID periods. The peak shifted from late afternoon before COVID to early morning during the pandemic, reflecting changes in daily routines and windows of opportunity for crime.

How to read this chart: Each line represents the percentage of burglaries occurring at each hour of the day, split across three periods: Pre-COVID (2015-2020), COVID Period (2020-2021), and Post-COVID (2021-2025). Use the dropdown to filter by district and compare how the hourly pattern shifts across periods.

One of the most striking stories the data tells is not where burglaries happen, but when they happen. Far from random, incidents follow a clear rhythm, echoing Routine Activity Theory (Cohen & Felson, 1979): crime appears where potential targets, unguarded spaces, and motivated offenders intersect, and this intersection unfolds on a daily schedule.

Before COVID, the pattern was predictable. The afternoon, around 5 PM, emerged as the burglary sweet spot—when office and retail workers left, natural guardianship waned, and commercial areas became vulnerable. Southern district tells this story vividly: nearly 9% of its daily burglaries occurred at 17h, the sharpest spike in any single hour. Across the city, the rest of the day was comparatively calm.

Then the pandemic arrived, and the clock shifted. With empty streets and closed businesses, the afternoon lull disappeared. Burglaries migrated to the early-morning hours, roughly 0–4 AM, a pattern that dominated nearly every district. Northern district shows the change most dramatically: a once-flat daily curve now spikes sharply at 4 AM, capturing 8% of daily incidents—its highest single-hour share ever. Central follows a similar rhythm, with the early-morning peak overtaking the old 5 PM afternoon high. Richmond adds a twist: its peak moves to 3–4 AM, but also develops a secondary spike around 8–9 PM.

Tenderloin, true to form, marches to its own beat. During COVID, a spike appears at 4 AM, but there’s also a smaller afternoon peak. Post-COVID, a surprising lunchtime cluster emerges around 13h, diverging from the citywide trend. With fewer overall burglaries, the district’s hourly patterns remain irregular, underscoring that even within the same city, routine disruption affects neighborhoods differently.

Conclusion

Burglary as a Window Into Urban Change

Burglary in San Francisco did not follow the general crime trend during COVID-19 — instead it moved against it. While most incident types declined sharply in 2020, burglary rose 52.7% citywide, with Northern district nearly doubling in a single year. This divergence is consistent with what Yim & Riddell (2022) found across multiple US cities: lockdowns emptied commercial spaces and removed the everyday guardianship that normally deters opportunistic burglary.

Burglary concentrated in districts with dense commercial corridors — Northern, Central, and Southern. Tenderloin was largely spared, with low burglary rates persisting even at the height of the pandemic. The hourly distribution shifted too: the pre-COVID afternoon peak around 17h gave way to an early-morning concentration at 3–4 AM during and after COVID, a pattern visible across most districts. Both shifts fit Routine Activity Theory (Cohen & Felson, 1979): as daily routines changed, so did the windows of opportunity.

Since 2022, burglary has declined sharply, dropping 27.6% in 2025 alone, reaching its lowest recorded level across the entire ten-year period. What drove this reversal is not visible in this data alone. Whether it reflects enforcement changes, post-pandemic urban recovery, or broader shifts in opportunity structures remains an open question.

A note on what this data can and cannot show

As Richardson et al. (2019) remind us, incident data captures what police record — not what actually happens. The patterns identified here are a starting point, not the full picture. The COVID spike in burglary could partly reflect changed reporting behavior: with more people at home, residents may have been more likely to notice and report break-ins that would previously have gone undetected. Conversely, the post-COVID decline could partly reflect shifts in enforcement priorities rather than genuine behavioral change.

If someone were to use this analysis to argue for a policy change — say, redeploying patrol resources away from Tenderloin toward Northern district — the data would not straightforwardly support that conclusion. Tenderloin's low burglary count does not mean it needs fewer resources overall; it reflects a different crime profile, not a safer neighborhood. Be skeptical of any claim that treats these patterns as the whole story.
